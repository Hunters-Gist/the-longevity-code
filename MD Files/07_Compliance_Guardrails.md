# The Sila Code Compliance Guardrails

## 1. Purpose Of This Document

This document defines the compliance guardrails for The Sila Code.

The Sila Code operates in a high-trust, high-risk category: health, wellness, longevity, performance, aesthetics, recovery, products, retreats, education, practitioner-guided pathways, and potentially regulated health-related offerings.

The brand must be built with compliance discipline from the beginning.

This document is not legal advice.

It is a working brand and content safety framework designed to reduce obvious risk, protect trust, and ensure The Sila Code does not drift into reckless claims, misleading advertising, or unsafe positioning.

Before launching regulated products, prescription-supported pathways, peptide-related content, therapeutic goods, health services, practitioner referrals, or medical-adjacent offers, The Sila Code should obtain professional legal and regulatory advice in Australia.

## 2. Core Compliance Principle

The central compliance principle is:

**Never make the brand sound more medical, more proven, more guaranteed, or more authorised than it actually is.**

The Sila Code should be:

- Education-first
- Lifestyle-first
- Evidence-informed
- Practitioner-aware
- Legally cautious
- Clear about limitations
- Honest about what is known and unknown
- Careful with health outcomes
- Careful with product claims
- Careful with testimonials
- Careful with before-and-after content
- Careful with prescription or research-use language

The brand should never rely on legal loophole thinking.

## 3. The Dangerous Trap

The dangerous trap is thinking:

> We can say anything if we add “research purposes only” or “not medical advice.”

That is weak, risky thinking.

Disclaimers help, but they do not save misleading, reckless, or illegal advertising.

If the overall impression of the website, product page, ad, caption, testimonial, image, funnel, email, or landing page suggests a prohibited or misleading claim, a disclaimer at the bottom may not fix it.

The entire customer impression matters.

## 4. Core Brand Compliance Position

The safest current position for The Sila Code is:

> The Sila Code provides lifestyle-focused education, self-optimisation frameworks, retreats, digital tools, content, and carefully positioned wellness pathways designed to support better habits, recovery, performance, appearance, and long-term vitality.

The brand should not position itself as:

- A medical clinic
- A pharmacy
- A peptide supplier
- A hormone clinic
- A diagnostic service
- A treatment provider
- A cure provider
- A prescription marketplace
- A disease-prevention service
- A guaranteed transformation system

Unless those structures are legally reviewed, properly licensed, and operationally compliant.

## 12. Public Advertising Rule For Prescription Medicines

The Sila Code must be extremely careful with prescription-only medicines, hormones, peptides, compounded medicines, and any therapeutic goods that cannot be advertised to the public.

General rule:

Do not publicly advertise prescription-only products or suggest that The Sila Code can help users obtain them unless a lawyer and qualified regulatory advisor have reviewed the model.

Avoid:

- Naming prescription medicines on public product pages
- Promoting prescription-only goods in public ads
- Using before-and-after content tied to prescription products
- Suggesting users can access prescriptions through the brand
- Creating public funnels that imply prescription supply
- Framing a health service as a way to obtain prescription medicines
- Using social media content that indirectly promotes prescription-only goods

Safer approach:

- Keep public content education-led
- Discuss broad categories, not supply
- Refer users to qualified healthcare professionals
- Separate general wellness content from any regulated pathway
- Do not advertise restricted goods to the public
- Get professional legal review before building any prescription-related pathway

## 13. Peptide And Hormone Guardrails

Peptides, hormones, and prescription-supported optimisation are high-risk.

Until legal review is complete, The Sila Code should not:

- Sell peptides
- Advertise peptides
- Recommend peptide protocols
- Publish peptide stacks
- Claim peptide benefits
- Suggest peptides are safe
- Suggest peptides are suitable for users
- Offer hormone protocols
- Claim hormone optimisation outcomes
- Suggest users can access prescription hormones through The Sila Code
- Use public social media to promote prescription-only substances
- Mix lifestyle education with implied prescription supply

If this area is explored later, the model must be designed around:

- Qualified medical professionals
- Clear separation between education and clinical services
- Proper licensing and legal review
- No public advertising of prescription-only goods
- Proper privacy handling
- Medical intake handled by appropriate professionals
- Clear clinical responsibility
- Clear patient consent, where applicable
- Proper adverse event and safety processes
- Written legal sign-off before launch

## 14. “Research Purposes Only” Guardrails

The phrase “research purposes only” must not be used as a lazy shield.

If a product is genuinely for research purposes, the business model, customer type, website language, purchasing process, fulfilment, warnings, restrictions, and documentation must align with that position.

Do not create a consumer wellness brand while pretending consumer-intended products are only for research.

Avoid:

- Selling research-use products to general consumers
- Using lifestyle transformation language around research-use items
- Pairing research-use products with body, anti-aging, or performance claims
- Using social proof or testimonials for research-use outcomes
- Suggesting human use
- Suggesting dosage, cycles, or protocols
- Using consumer-friendly “benefit” language

Safer rule:

If the business wants to explore research-use products, get legal advice before writing a single product page.

## 15. Therapeutic Goods Guardrails

If a product is, or could be perceived as, a therapeutic good, The Sila Code must check:

- Whether it is regulated
- Whether it needs to be listed, registered, or otherwise compliant
- Whether the claims are permitted
- Whether advertising to the public is allowed
- Whether the label is compliant
- Whether ingredients are permitted
- Whether warnings are required
- Whether testimonials are allowed
- Whether influencers can talk about it
- Whether the website, email, social, and product page language is compliant

Do not assume a product is safe to market just because competitors are doing it.

Competitors being reckless does not make the strategy legal.

## 37. Content Review Checklist

Before publishing any page, ad, caption, email, product description, retreat page, or sales page, check:

1. Does it make a medical claim?
2. Does it imply diagnosis, treatment, cure, reversal, or prevention?
3. Does it mention prescription medicines, peptides, or hormones?
4. Does it imply guaranteed results?
5. Does it use unsafe before-and-after content?
6. Does it rely on testimonials about clinical outcomes?
7. Does it claim something is proven without evidence?
8. Does it make a product sound safer than it is?
9. Does it use fake urgency or scarcity?
10. Does the disclaimer match the actual content?
11. Would a reasonable consumer think this is a therapeutic claim?
12. Would a regulator see this as advertising a therapeutic good?
13. Does it need legal review before publishing?

If the answer to any high-risk question is yes, stop and review.
